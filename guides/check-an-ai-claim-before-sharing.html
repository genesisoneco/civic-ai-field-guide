Verification habit

How to check an AI claim before sharing it.

A claim about AI can be exciting, frightening, or useful — and still be incomplete. Use this quick field check before you repost, buy, ban, adopt, or panic.

The 90-second version

  1. Name the exact claim. What is being said: capability, risk, product promise, benchmark result, legal change, or social impact?
  2. Find the original source. Prefer the paper, official documentation, regulator notice, dataset card, model card, or direct demo over a summary thread.
  3. Check the conditions. What task, language, dataset, hardware, date, safety filter, human review, or lab setting was involved?
  4. Look for independent confirmation. One demo is not the same as broad reliability.
  5. Ask what action follows. If the only action is outrage, awe, or purchase, slow down.

A practical checklist

1. Translate the headline into a testable sentence

Replace vague words with a concrete claim. “AI can replace doctors” becomes: “This system can diagnose condition X, for population Y, with accuracy Z, in setting Q, without unsafe delay.”

This matters because trustworthy AI depends on context. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework emphasizes characteristics such as validity, reliability, safety, security, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness — not a single magic score.

2. Separate capability from deployment

An AI system may perform well on a benchmark and still fail in real use because the users, incentives, data, environment, or error costs are different. Ask:

  • Was this a lab result, a production system, or a marketing demo?
  • Were humans reviewing outputs before harm could occur?
  • What happens when the system is uncertain, wrong, overloaded, or attacked?

3. Treat screenshots and demos as weak evidence

A screenshot can show that something happened once. It usually does not show base rates, failure cases, cherry-picking, hidden prompts, post-editing, or whether the result repeats. Prefer reproducible examples, documented evaluations, audits, or official incident reports.

4. Check who benefits if you believe it

AI claims can be shaped by vendors, investors, activists, institutions, creators, competitors, or frightened communities. Incentives do not automatically make a claim false, but they tell you what to verify carefully. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission has warned companies not to overstate AI product capabilities or make claims without adequate evidence.

5. Match the evidence standard to the stakes

  • Low stakes: a productivity tip may only need a quick trial and a privacy check.
  • Medium stakes: school, workplace, or civic decisions need clear limits, source trails, and human accountability.
  • High stakes: health, finance, housing, criminal justice, immigration, and safety claims need domain experts, formal validation, governance, and documented recourse.

For health uses, the World Health Organization’s AI ethics guidance stresses human autonomy, safety, transparency, accountability, equity, and sustainable public interest — a higher bar than “the demo looked good.”

6. Look for missing comparison points

Ask “compared with what?” A claim that a model is better is incomplete unless it says better than which baseline, on which metric, with which tradeoffs, and for whom.

7. Decide what you can responsibly say

If the evidence is thin, share the uncertainty instead of the claim. Useful phrases:

  • “This appears to be a single demo, not proof of general reliability.”
  • “The original source says X; the viral post adds Y, which I cannot verify.”
  • “This may be worth watching, but I would not use it for high-stakes decisions yet.”

Red flags

  • “AI proves…” without a source, date, method, or uncertainty.
  • Benchmarks presented without dataset details or real-world limitations.
  • “This changes everything” language with no concrete user decision attached.
  • Claims that a tool is “safe,” “unbiased,” “sentient,” or “human-level” without definitions.
  • Pressure to buy, ban, deploy, or panic immediately.
